1. FUNCTIONAL FORM SELECTION

We model GPU price depreciation as a univariate function P(t) where t is
months since launch and P is the price ratio (current price / MSRP). The
dependent variable is normalised by MSRP so that all GPUs share a common
scale regardless of price tier, and so that P(0) = 1.0 by construction.

Two candidate functional forms were selected:

    Exponential with floor:   P(t) = f + (1 - f) * exp(-k * t)
    Power law with floor:     P(t) = f + (1 - f) * (t + 1)^(-b)

where f is the long-run residual value floor, k is the exponential decay
rate, and b is the power-law exponent.

Both forms enforce P(0) = 1.0 by construction. The exponential model implies
a constant proportional rate of value loss per unit time -- the asset loses
a fixed fraction of its remaining above-floor value each month. The
power-law model implies a declining rate -- depreciation is steepest
immediately after launch and attenuates over time. Both converge to the
floor parameter f as t approaches infinity.

The power-law model was reparameterised from its original three-parameter
form (f + a * (t+1)^(-b)) to eliminate the amplitude parameter a by setting
a = 1 - f. The original form had a degree of freedom that allowed
unphysical launch values (e.g., P(0) = f + a, which could exceed 1.0 when
a > 1 - f). The reparameterisation removes this pathological regime and
reduces the parameter count from three to two, making both candidate models
equal in complexity.

Why these two forms and not others: exponential decay is the standard model
in the fixed-asset depreciation literature (Hulten & Wykoff, 1981). Power-law
decay is used by the Bureau of Economic Analysis for certain asset classes
and captures the empirical observation that depreciation decelerates over
time. We considered but rejected linear decay (unphysical -- it implies the
GPU reaches zero value at a finite time and then goes negative), logistic
decay (three parameters, difficult to identify from sparse data), and
spline-based approaches (overfit small samples). The two-model ensemble
provides model-form hedging without the parameter explosion of more flexible
specifications.


2. PARAMETER BOUNDS

Parameters are bounded during optimisation to prevent physically
implausible fits:

    floor f:  [0.001, 0.80]
    k:        [0.005, 0.25]
    b:        [0.01, 3.0]

The floor bounds allow residual values from 0.1% (near-worthless) to 80%.
The upper bound was lowered from 0.95 to 0.80, and the lower bound from
0.01 to 0.001, to better accommodate GPUs at both extremes of the
depreciation spectrum. The decay rate k = 0.005 corresponds to a half-life
of approximately 138 months, effectively flat over any five-year horizon.
k = 0.25 gives a half-life of 2.8 months -- the fastest plausible
depreciation for any GPU in a functioning secondary market.

These bounds were tightened from an earlier specification of k in [1e-4, 1.0].
The original range permitted half-lives of 579 years (lower bound) and
sub-one-month (upper bound), both unphysical. The revised bounds were
informed by the empirical range of fitted parameters across the dataset and
the constraint that any result should correspond to a recognisable
depreciation trajectory.


3. FITTING PROCEDURE

Models are fit by nonlinear least squares via the Levenberg-Marquardt
algorithm (scipy.optimize.curve_fit, maxfev = 12000). The objective function
uses proportional (relative) error weighting:

    sigma_i = max(y_i, 0.05)

where y_i is the observed price ratio at time i. This makes the fitting
criterion scale-invariant: a 10% deviation from the curve is penalised
equally whether the GPU is at 80% of MSRP or at 20%. Without proportional
weighting, high-value observations would dominate the fit and low-value
tail observations would be effectively ignored. The floor of 0.05 prevents
observations near zero from receiving unbounded leverage.

The curve_fit call uses absolute_sigma = False, which means scipy
internally rescales the covariance matrix by the reduced chi-squared
statistic (chi2 / (n - p)). This is important: it inflates the covariance
when the model is a poor fit (large residuals), which propagates into wider
Monte Carlo uncertainty intervals downstream. The alternative
(absolute_sigma = True) would treat the sigma_i as exact noise levels and
produce overconfident covariance estimates when the model is misspecified.

After curve_fit returns, the covariance matrix is inflated by a Bartlett
serial-correlation correction factor. The lag-1 autocorrelation rho of the
weighted residuals is estimated, and pcov is multiplied by (1+rho)/(1-rho).
This accounts for the effective-sample-size reduction caused by temporal
autocorrelation in monthly GPU price observations. For typical rho values
of 0.3-0.7, this widens confidence intervals by a factor of 1.4-2.4x
(sqrt of the covariance inflation).

R-squared is computed in the same weighted space:

    R2 = 1 - SS_res_w / SS_tot_w

where SS_res_w = sum((y_i - pred_i)^2 / sigma_i^2) and
SS_tot_w = sum((y_i - y_bar_w)^2 / sigma_i^2), with y_bar_w being the
weighted mean. This ensures the R-squared value is consistent with the
fitting objective and the AICc computation that follows.


4. MODEL SELECTION: AICc-WEIGHTED ENSEMBLE

Rather than selecting a single model, we compute Akaike weights from the
corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) and blend the two models in
proportion to their relative support from the data.

The AICc formula under the proportional-weight error model is:

    AICc = SS_res_w + 2*k + 2*k*(k+1) / (n - k - 1)

where k is the number of curve parameters (k = 2 for both models) and n
is the number of observations. The correction term (Hurvich & Tsai, 1989)
adjusts for small-sample bias in the standard AIC. Because both models share
the same data and the same sigma_i weights, the constant terms in the
Gaussian log-likelihood (involving sum of log(sigma_i)) cancel in the AIC
differences and are omitted.

Under this error model the noise variance is not a separately estimated
parameter -- the sigma_i are treated as known weights -- so k_eff = k
(the count of curve parameters only), not k + 1.

Akaike weights are computed as:

    delta_i = AICc_i - AICc_min
    w_i     = exp(-0.5 * delta_i) / sum_j(exp(-0.5 * delta_j))

following Burnham & Anderson (2002). When both models fit comparably, the
ensemble gives roughly equal weight to each; when one clearly dominates, the
ensemble is effectively single-model.

In practice, we found that the power-law model dominated for most consumer
and workstation GPUs (receiving weight > 0.99 in many cases), while the
exponential and power-law split more evenly for datacenter GPUs with longer
histories. The ensemble thus acts as an automatic model selector for GPUs
where one form clearly fits better, while providing genuine blending for
GPUs where the evidence is ambiguous.

Quality gates precede the ensemble:

  Gate 1: any model with R-squared below 0.20 is excluded before computing
  weights. If both models fall below this threshold, the ensemble returns
  no result and the GPU falls through to the next fitting tier.

  Gate 2: MC median at t=0 must be <= 1.5 (back-extrapolation does not
  imply an implausible above-MSRP launch price).

  Gate 3 (boundary-hit detection): if any fitted parameter is pinned at
  its bound (within tolerance 1e-4 of the bound range), the fit is flagged
  as degenerate. This prevents boundary artifacts from producing Tier 1
  results that appear well-fit (high R-squared) but are not meaningful.
  For segment canonical fits, a floor <= 0.04 triggers the degenerate
  detection (raised from 0.02).

If any gate fails, the GPU falls through to Tier 2.


5. THREE-TIER HIERARCHICAL FITTING

5.0 CONDITION SWITCH

Before curve fitting, the pipeline applies a condition-based data selection
rule. For the first 24 months of each GPU's life (t <= 24), only NEW-condition
price observations are used. After month 24 (t > 24), the pipeline
switches to REFURBISHED and USED condition data. This reflects the market
reality that new-condition units dominate early secondary-market listings,
while refurbished and used units become the primary transaction type as
hardware ages past two years.

Six GPUs currently lack refurbished/used data entirely: H100 PCIe 80 GB,
H100 SXM5 94 GB, H200 SXM, Gaudi2, H200 NVL, and GH200. For these GPUs,
the pipeline falls back to NEW-condition data for months 24+. As
refurbished/used listings accumulate for these models, the fallback will
no longer be needed.

Not every GPU has sufficient data for a robust individual fit. We built a
three-tier system that gracefully degrades as data decreases:

TIER 1 (INDIVIDUAL FIT): Requires 18+ monthly observations beginning
before month 30 of the GPU's life. Both candidate models are fit
independently to the GPU's own price history. The AICc ensemble produces the
final projection and Monte Carlo uncertainty. Note: the observed method
(direct use of the GPU's price history without parametric curve fitting)
now serves as a fallback within Tier 1 when individual fits fail quality
gates, rather than as a peer method alongside fitted results.

TIER 2 (CONSTRAINED FIT): For GPUs with 4-17 months of data, or where
Tier 1 fits failed quality gates. The decay rate k is fixed to the segment
canonical value, and only the floor parameter f is estimated from the GPU's
individual observations, regularised by an empirical Bayes prior. For
Tier 2 constrained fits, the projection is anchored at the GPU's current
observed market price (no cap -- if a GPU trades above MSRP, the forward
projection starts from that elevated level and decays from there) rather
than projecting from the theoretical launch price. This conditional
forecasting approach produces more realistic projections for GPUs that have
already depreciated significantly or are trading above MSRP.

TIER 3 (SEGMENT DEFAULT): For GPUs with fewer than 4 observations or where
all fits failed. The GPU receives the segment canonical curve scaled to its
current observed price level.

The tier assignment is strictly hierarchical. In our results, twenty-eight
of sixty GPUs qualified for Tier 1 (observed or individual fit),
twenty-eight fell to Tier 2 (constrained), and four landed in Tier 3
(segment default). Eight GPUs were flagged as appreciating and received
Tier 1 treatment but with LOW confidence due to the anomalous price
direction.


6. SEGMENT CANONICAL CURVES

GPUs are partitioned into three market segments -- datacenter, workstation,
and consumer -- each with a canonical depreciation curve fit to pooled
monthly observations of all well-observed GPUs in that segment. The pooled
fitting approach weights each GPU proportionally to its number of monthly
observations.

The datacenter canonical is now data-driven, anchored by the T4 (77 months
of history), V100 variants, A10, A16, A30, and A100 PCIe variants. The
consumer and workstation canonicals produced somewhat faster decay.

Fallback values (SEG_K_FALLBACK) are provided for cases where the
canonical fit is degenerate (R-squared < 0, k at upper bound, or floor
<= 0.04). Current fallback values, informed by long-run eBay sold data:

    DATACENTER:  k = 0.025, floor = 0.02
        (K80 at 0.4-0.7%, V100 at 3-4% after 8-9 years)
    CONSUMER:    k = 0.020, floor = 0.05
        (GTX 980 Ti at 8% after 11 years; absolute $25-50 floor)
    WORKSTATION: k = 0.018, floor = 0.03
        (Quadro M6000 at 1.9%, K6000 at 1-2% after 11-13 years)

When the pre-successor-only canonical fit is degenerate, the pipeline
retries with all data (including post-successor observations). If the
retry also fails (R-squared < 0.20 in strict mode), the handcrafted
SEG_K_FALLBACK is used.


7. EMPIRICAL BAYES FLOOR PRIOR

For Tier 2 GPUs that have not yet shown visible depreciation (notably the
H100 at ages 17-35 months), the floor parameter is weakly identified: the
optimiser hits the upper bound of 0.95 because the short observation window
shows no decline.

We address this with a penalised WLS objective:

    L(f) = SS_res_w(f) + lambda * (f - mu_prior)^2 / sigma_prior^2

where lambda = max(0, 1 - n_eff / 36) ramps from full prior influence at
zero months to pure data-driven estimation at 36+ months.

For the datacenter segment, we computed a prior of mean = 0.491,
sigma = 0.235, based on seven anchor GPUs with 48+ months of history. This
prior was decisive for the H100 family: without it, H100 PCIe 80 GB
returned TVR = 0.950 (the parameter boundary, not a real estimate). With
the prior, the floor is pulled toward the 0.35-0.50 range, producing a
more informative projection. Combined with the Tier 2 forward projection
(which anchors at the current observed price rather than MSRP), the H100
variants now produce TVRs well below the 0.950 boundary. The prior
deliberately does not dominate the data, but the forward projection
methodology provides the additional constraint needed to break through the
boundary for GPUs still trading near MSRP.


8. SUCCESSOR ANNOUNCEMENT ADJUSTMENT

GPU prices exhibit a discrete step-change of 20-40% when the successor
generation is publicly announced. We maintain a database of fifty successor
announcement dates and fit Tier 1 curves using only pre-announcement data
when 18+ months of pre-announcement observations are available.

This adjustment is critical for the RTX 30-series. The RTX 3080 launched
September 2020 and its successor (RTX 40-series) was announced September
2022, giving exactly 24 months of pre-announcement data. Without the
adjustment, the fitted k would absorb the September 2022 price drop and
overstate the structural decay rate. With the adjustment, the pre-
announcement k reflects pure depreciation, and the announcement effect is
isolated. We found that the successor step-change accounted for approximately
20-35% of the total price decline in the RTX 30-series fitting window.


9. MONTE CARLO UNCERTAINTY PROPAGATION

Uncertainty is quantified via 10,000 Monte Carlo draws (increased from
5,000) from the fitted parameter covariance matrix. Each draw samples
parameters from N(theta_hat, Sigma_hat), evaluates the model, and the
ensemble combines using fixed Akaike weights. The covariance is regularised
with a ridge term (1e-6 * |trace|) and a condition number gate (threshold
1e10).

The final projection is the median of the 10,000 draws. The 95% credible
interval uses the Highest Posterior Density (HPD) method -- the shortest
interval containing 95% of samples -- rather than equal-tailed percentiles.
HPD is preferred for skewed posteriors (common near parameter bounds) and
keeps the point estimate near the centre of the band. sigma_yr5 is the
sample standard deviation of the t = 60 column. p_below_10pct is now
reported at three horizons: 5-year (t=60), 8-year (t=96), and 10-year
(t=120).

Projections extend to 120 months via CURVE_TIMES = [0, 12, 24, 36, 48,
60, 72, 84, 96, 108, 120]. The output CSV includes proj_t72 through
proj_t120 columns alongside the original proj_t0 through proj_t60.

For Tier 2, floor and k are sampled jointly with cross-covariance derived
from the segment canonical correlation structure, preserving the negative
floor-k correlation that is intrinsic to the exponential model. Tier 2 and
Tier 3 scale factors include +/-20% stochastic noise (uniform) to reflect
uncertainty in the current observed price level, with scale clips widened
from [0.5, 2.0] to [0.3, 2.5].

Half-life for Tier 2/3 GPUs now uses _half_life_from_curve() (linear
interpolation on the projected curve) instead of _half_life_exp() (the
analytical formula). Half-life values below 1.0 month are treated as NaN
(boundary artifact at t=0).

13. REFURBISHMENT PREMIUM RESULTS

The confirmatory OLS test found a refurbishment coefficient of +0.177 on
the log-price scale, translating to a premium of +19.4% over used-condition
GPUs (95% CI: +14.9% to +24.1%, p < 0.0001). The test controlled for GPU
model and time period via fixed effects, with cluster-robust standard errors
at the GPU level (~54 clusters).

The overall model R-squared is 0.885, but this is dominated by the GPU
fixed effects (a $300 RTX 4060 and a $25,000 H100 are in the same
regression). The condition coefficient explains the within-GPU, within-month
price gap between used and refurbished listings -- a much smaller fraction
of total variance, but a statistically well-identified one.

The cluster bootstrap (2000 iterations, GPU-level resampling) produced a
wider 95% CI that may include zero. This is expected: the bootstrap
estimates the unconditional median-of-medians premium without controlling
for GPU or time, which is a different and noisier estimand than the
conditional OLS coefficient. The disagreement reflects GPU-level
heterogeneity, not weakness in the OLS result.

Per-GPU exploratory t-tests (Welch, unequal variance) with Benjamini-
Hochberg FDR correction at q = 0.10 showed 39 of 45 testable GPUs with
individually significant positive premiums. Power analysis (minimum
detectable Cohen's d = 0.30 at 80% power) excluded 9 GPUs from testing
due to insufficient sample sizes.

The magnitude of the premium varies substantially across GPUs. Some
datacenter cards show premiums exceeding 40%, while some consumer cards
show premiums near zero or slightly negative. The +19.4% figure is the
market-wide average conditional on GPU and time -- individual GPU premiums
should be evaluated using the per-GPU decomposition.

17. KNOWN STATISTICAL LIMITATIONS

Serial correlation in monthly data means the raw covariance from curve_fit
underestimates parameter uncertainty. We apply a Bartlett correction factor
(1+rho)/(1-rho) to pcov, where rho is the estimated lag-1 autocorrelation
of the weighted residuals. This inflates the covariance to approximate the
effective-sample-size reduction from autocorrelation, widening the MC
confidence intervals. For typical GPU monthly data with rho in [0.3, 0.7],
this inflates pcov by a factor of 1.9-5.7x. The correction is applied in
fit_model() before the covariance enters the MC pipeline. This partially
addresses the serial correlation issue but is a first-order approximation;
a full Newey-West or HAC correction would be more rigorous.

Neither the exponential nor the power-law form may be the correct
functional form. The AICc ensemble hedges between two candidates but does
not account for the possibility that both are wrong.

The dataset only captures GPUs that are actively trading. The estimated
price floor represents tradeable market value, not liquidation or scrap
value.

The pipeline's thresholds were refined during development against the data.
While each choice is individually motivated, sensitivity analysis across
threshold values has been performed and shows the ranking is robust
(Spearman rho > 0.965 across all tested threshold variations). Results
should be interpreted with this context.

The H100 and H200 projections, which are the most commercially important
numbers in the analysis, are also the least statistically reliable. This is
an inherent tension: the GPUs with the highest stakes are the ones with the
least depreciation history. The empirical Bayes prior partially addresses
this by borrowing strength from predecessor GPUs, but a 17-month-old GPU
that has never declined in price simply does not contain enough information
to project a five-year trajectory with confidence.

18a. DATA QUALITY IMPROVEMENTS (SINCE INITIAL RELEASE)

The following data quality improvements were made to the pipeline:

  V100 FHHL aliasing: The Tesla V100 FHHL (low-profile PCIe form factor,
  same GV100 die) is aliased to Tesla V100 PCIe 16 GB via GPU_NAME_ALIASES
  to consolidate what is the same product under one price history.

  SXM carrier cost: Used V100 SXM2 bare modules (sold without the carrier
  board needed for server installation) receive a $200 carrier cost
  adjustment (CARRIER_COST = 200) to reflect the "usable equivalent"
  price. Without this, bare SXM2 modules artificially deflate the V100
  depreciation floor.

  US-only filter: The retail CSVs contain multi-country data with prices in
  local currency mislabeled as USD. Non-US datacenter GPU prices are 2-5x
  US levels. Filtering to US-only reduces the dataset from ~580K to ~158K
  rows. Keepa data is already US-only.

  Title-based junk filter: 12 junk keywords (e.g. "as is", "for parts",
  "defective"), 6 accessory keywords (e.g. "riser cable", "gpu holder"),
  and 4 server bundle keywords (e.g. "dgx", "server node", "4x a100")
  remove non-GPU products and multi-GPU bundles from the dataset.

  Keepa renewed/refurbished product filter and max age filter (72 months).

  Age-tiered USED price caps: 24-48 months: 1.5x MSRP, 48-72 months:
  0.75x MSRP, 72+ months: 0.50x MSRP. Removes server bundles and
  mislabeled form factors (SXM4 sold as "PCIe") from used data.

  NEW fallback requires >= 3 distinct monthly periods per GPU. Prevents a
  single stale Amazon listing repeated across months from creating phantom
  data.

  Supplement prediction/estimate row filter: rows from other pricing models
  (e.g. "estimate", "age-based", "consensus") are excluded to prevent
  circular dependency.

  Outlier floor lowered from $200 to $50 so cheap consumer GPUs can show
  realistic depreciation below 67% of MSRP.

  MSRP corrections: L4 = $2,500 (was $7,000 OEM bundle), A40 = $5,000
  (was $27,500 Cisco GPL OEM), L40 = $7,500 (was $31,000 OEM),
  A16 = $2,800 (was $9,558 SHI reseller OEM).

18c. HIERARCHICAL BAYESIAN MODEL (SUPPLEMENTARY)

hierarchical_bayes.py implements a PyMC-based hierarchical model as a
supplementary validation of the primary scipy pipeline. The model places
segment-level hyperpriors on the decay parameters (k, floor) and allows
GPU-level partial pooling via:

    k_segment ~ HalfNormal(sigma_k)
    floor_segment ~ Beta(alpha_floor, beta_floor)
    k_gpu ~ Normal(k_segment, sigma_k_gpu)
    floor_gpu ~ Normal(floor_segment, sigma_floor_gpu)

Key results:
  - 0.948 Pearson correlation with scipy pipeline TVR estimates.
  - Better estimates for sparse-data GPUs (e.g., MI300X, GH200) where
    partial pooling produces more informative posteriors than the Tier 3
    segment default.
  - The hierarchical model naturally handles the "borrowing strength"
    problem that the empirical Bayes prior addresses for Tier 2 GPUs.
  - Results available in outputs/hierarchical_bayes_results.csv.


18d. MONTE CARLO VALIDATION

mc_convergence_test.py validates MC sample size adequacy by comparing 2,000
vs 10,000 samples on five representative GPUs (one per fitting pathway:
Tier 1 fitted, Tier 1 observed, Tier 2 constrained, Tier 3 default, and
demand-shocked). It measures the coefficient of variation of CI percentiles
across ten runs with different random seeds. Result: 10,000 samples produce
stable CI estimates (CV < 1% for all CI boundaries).

test_mc_symmetry.py tests whether the MC posterior predictive is
approximately symmetric in log-space (justifying the normal approximation
for uncertainty bands). For each test GPU at each time point
(t = 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60), it compares level-space vs log-space
skewness and CI arm asymmetry. The test monkey-patches _mc_to_stats()
to intercept the raw MC arrays for ~10 representative GPUs.


18d-ii. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS (sensitivity_analysis.py)

sensitivity_analysis.py varies four key pipeline thresholds one at a time
and measures how much the depreciation rankings change. It monkey-patches
module constants before each run and restores defaults afterward.

Thresholds tested:

    TIER1_MIN_MONTHS:    [12, 15, 18, 21, 24]    (default: 18)
    OBS_WINDOW:          [6, 9, 12, 15, 18]       (default: 12)
    APPRECIATING_THR:    [1.05, 1.1, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0]  (default: 1.2)
    TIER1_MAX_MIN_AGE:   [18, 24, 30, 36, 42]     (default: 30)

For each scenario, the full pipeline (fit_all_gpus + build_ranking) is

HOW TO READ THESE RESULTS

TVR is the fraction of the original price you get back after 5 years.

  TVR 0.15  =  you lose 85% of what you paid  ($30,000 GPU worth $4,500)
  TVR 0.50  =  you lose 50%                   ($30,000 GPU worth $15,000)
  TVR 0.85  =  you lose 15%                   ($30,000 GPU worth $25,500)
  TVR 1.00  =  you break even
  TVR 1.15  =  the GPU appreciated 15%        ($30,000 GPU worth $34,500)

To calculate expected dollar loss for any GPU:

  Dollar loss = MSRP x (1 - TVR)

  Example: H100 SXM5 64 GB at TVR 0.267
           $25,000 x (1 - 0.267) = $25,000 x 0.733 = ~$18,325 lost

The 95% CI (HPD interval) gives you the range. For that same H100 SXM5 64:
  Best case (CI high = 0.559):  lose $11,025 (retain $13,975)
  Expected (TVR = 0.267):       lose $18,325 (retain $6,675)
  Worst case (CI low = 0.057):  lose $23,575 (retain $1,425)

sigma tells you how spread out the projection is. Think of it as:
  sigma < 0.05:   very tight -- trust the number
  sigma 0.05-0.15: moderate spread -- directionally reliable
  sigma 0.15-0.30: wide spread -- use for rough planning only
  sigma > 0.30:    extremely uncertain -- treat as a guess

GPUs with TVR above 1.0 are currently trading above their launch MSRP.
This is unusual and reflects AI-driven demand. These values will almost
certainly decline as supply normalises -- do not treat TVR > 1.0 as a
reliable long-term forecast.
