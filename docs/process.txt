GPU SECONDARY MARKET DEPRECIATION ANALYSIS
Process and Results
March 2026


THE PROBLEM

Graphics processing units are capital assets. A company that buys an NVIDIA
H100 for thirty thousand dollars or an RTX 4090 for sixteen hundred dollars
needs to know what that hardware will be worth in three years, or five. The
same question matters to anyone buying used or refurbished GPUs: are you
getting a fair price, or overpaying for hardware that is about to become
worthless?

There is no Kelley Blue Book for GPUs. No standard depreciation table. No
widely accepted model. Pricing is opaque, scattered across eBay listings,
Amazon storefronts, enterprise resellers, and private deals. This project
set out to build that model from scratch -- to collect real transaction data,
fit mathematical depreciation curves to each GPU, and produce a ranking of
which GPUs hold their value and which do not.


THE DATA

We started with roughly three hundred thousand cleaned price observations
covering sixty GPU models. The data came from two sources.

The first source was scraped retail and secondary-market listings from Amazon
and eBay, covering June 2024 through mid-March 2026. Every observation
included the GPU model name, the date, the condition (new, used, or
refurbished), the price in US dollars, and the original manufacturer's
suggested retail price.

Twenty-two months of data is not enough to model five-year depreciation. To
extend the history, we incorporated Amazon price tracking data from a service
called Keepa, which silently records every Amazon price change going back
years. This gave us pricing histories stretching back to each GPU's original
launch date -- as far back as 2017 for models like the Tesla V100. For
consumer GPUs like the RTX 3080, which launched in September 2020, we ended
up with roughly sixty-six months of continuous price history.

The limitation is datacenter GPUs. Models like the A100 and H100 are sold
through enterprise channels, not Amazon. Keepa has no record of them. To
address this, we built several supplementary data pipelines:

eBay completed/sold listings (scrape_ebay_sold.py) target fifteen thin-data
datacenter GPUs -- H100, H200, MI300X, Gaudi2, and others -- extracting
actual transaction prices from eBay's sold listings database. Each listing
is scored for confidence and filtered for accessories, multi-GPU systems,
and extreme prices.

Vast.ai rental pricing (scrape_vastai.py) queries the public API for current
GPU cloud rental rates across all available hardware. Hourly rental rates
are converted to implied three-year asset values as a cross-check for the
depreciation models.

Cloud GPU rental history (extract_cloud_pricing.py) processes a historical
cloud pricing database spanning 2015 to 2024, extracting monthly rental
rate time series for datacenter GPUs. Cloud rental rate decline over time
serves as an independent signal of hardware depreciation.

TechPowerUp specifications (scrape_techpowerup.py) cross-checks the curated
MSRP and launch date constants against the TechPowerUp GPU database.

A web-scraped supplement provides additional secondary-market observations
from enterprise resellers and pricing aggregators.

Together, these supplements substantially improve datacenter GPU coverage.
Without them, datacenter GPUs rely on only the twenty-two-month retail
window, which makes their five-year projections less reliable. With the
full dataset, fifteen of sixty GPUs saw their projections shift by
more than ten percentage points, concentrated in the datacenter segment.
We are transparent about this throughout the analysis.

An important distinction: the data sources differ in whether they represent
executed transactions or listed/offered pricing. eBay completed/sold
listings are confirmed transactions -- actual prices paid by buyers. Amazon
retail listings and Keepa historical tracking are listed prices (offers
rather than confirmed sales), though Amazon's high sell-through rate means
listed and transacted prices are closely aligned for consumer GPUs. Cloud
rental rates and enterprise reseller prices are offered rates used as
cross-reference signals. The pipeline does not use indicative pricing from
other models or consensus estimates -- a specific filter removes any
supplementary rows flagged as "estimate," "age-based," or "consensus" to
prevent circular dependency.


CLEANING THE DATA

Raw scraped prices are messy. The same GPU appears with different condition
labels -- "New," "new," "NEW," "Renewed," "Open Box." Prices include
data-entry errors, joke listings, and scraping artifacts. Some GPUs are
currently selling above their original sticker price because of AI-driven
demand shortages.

We cleaned in stages. First we filtered to US-only data. The retail CSVs
contain multi-country listings (US, UK, SG, FR, FI, ES, etc.) with prices
in local currency mislabeled as USD. Non-US datacenter GPU prices are two
to five times US levels (for example, a French A100 at forty-two thousand
euros labeled as forty-two thousand dollars versus the actual US price of
eight thousand eight hundred). This filter removes roughly eighty-five
percent of rows but eliminates systematic price inflation for datacenter
GPUs. The Keepa data is already US-only.

Next we standardized every condition label into three categories: new, used,
and refurbished. Anything that did not map to one of those -- like "For
Parts" -- was discarded. Then we removed exact duplicate listings across
our overlapping data files.

We then applied a title-based junk filter. GPU listings attract non-GPU
products: dead cards sold "as is," heatsink-only kits, riser cables
mis-tagged as GPU products, and multi-GPU server bundles (DGX systems,
server nodes) priced as single GPUs. The filter uses twelve junk keywords,
six accessory keywords, and four server bundle keywords to remove these
before they can contaminate the pipeline. Keepa data also passes through a
renewed/refurbished product filter and a maximum age filter of seventy-two
months.

We established a single authoritative MSRP for each GPU using a curated
reference table of official launch prices (MSRP_REFERENCE) as the primary
source, with the median of all reported CSV MSRPs as a fallback for GPUs
not in the reference table. This eliminates the five-to-ten percent
variation that scraping introduces and corrects for reseller markups on
datacenter GPUs. Several MSRPs were corrected from OEM bundle prices to
standalone GPU prices: L4 from seven thousand to twenty-five hundred
dollars, A40 from twenty-seven thousand five hundred to five thousand
dollars, L40 from thirty-one thousand to seventy-five hundred dollars,
and A16 from ninety-five hundred to twenty-eight hundred dollars.

One GPU alias consolidation: the Tesla V100 FHHL (a low-profile form
factor of the V100 PCIe 16 GB using the same die and targeting the same
market) is aliased to the Tesla V100 PCIe 16 GB throughout the pipeline
to avoid splitting a single product's price history across two names.

For used V100 SXM2 bare modules, which are sold without the carrier board
needed to install them in a server, we add a two-hundred-dollar carrier
cost adjustment to reflect the true "usable equivalent" price. Without
this, bare SXM2 modules appear artificially cheap and deflate the V100's
depreciation floor.

We removed any listing priced below a floor of the greater of ten percent
of MSRP or fifty dollars, which catches scraper false positives
(wrong-product matches, cables, accessories) without removing legitimately
cheap old hardware. The floor was lowered from two hundred to fifty dollars
so that inexpensive consumer GPUs (like the RTX 4060 at two hundred ninety-
nine dollars MSRP) can show realistic depreciation below sixty-seven percent
of MSRP. We also capped prices at three times MSRP to remove multi-GPU
server bundles. For used and refurbished GPUs, age-tiered price caps apply:
one and a half times MSRP for ages twenty-four to forty-eight months,
seventy-five percent of MSRP for ages forty-eight to seventy-two months,
and fifty percent of MSRP for ages beyond seventy-two months.

The datacenter supplement (eBay sold listings, reseller prices) also filters
out prediction and estimate rows from other pricing models to prevent
circular dependency.

Finally, we flagged GPUs currently experiencing demand shocks. If a GPU's
recent new-condition median price exceeded one and a half times its MSRP over
the past ninety days, based on at least three observations, we flagged it.
These GPUs -- primarily the H100, H200, and several RTX 40-series models --
are included in the main depreciation ranking but flagged separately with
scenario analysis showing their projected trajectories under both
shock-persists and normalisation assumptions.


HOW WE BUILT THE DEPRECIATION CURVES

The core of the analysis is fitting a mathematical curve to each GPU's price
history and projecting it forward to the five-year mark. Every GPU's price
is expressed as a ratio of its original MSRP. A ratio of 0.40 means the GPU
retains forty percent of its launch price.

A key methodological choice is the condition switch. For the first
twenty-four months of each GPU's life (age less than or equal to twenty-four
months), the pipeline uses NEW-condition price data -- reflecting the period
when most units on the market are factory-fresh. After month twenty-four
(age strictly greater than twenty-four), the pipeline switches to
REFURBISHED or USED condition data, reflecting the reality that
secondary-market transactions increasingly involve non-new units as
hardware ages. For six GPUs where no refurbished
or used data exists (H100 PCIe 80 GB, H100 SXM5 94 GB, H200 SXM, Gaudi2,
H200 NVL, and GH200), the pipeline continues using NEW-condition data as a
fallback for months twenty-four and beyond.

We used two curve shapes. The first is exponential decay with a floor --
the GPU loses a fixed percentage of its remaining value each month, but the
price never drops below a minimum level because there are always some buyers
willing to pay something for working hardware. The second is power-law decay
with a floor -- a steeper initial drop that slows more gradually over time.
Both shapes are standard in asset depreciation literature and both enforce
that the GPU starts at one hundred percent of MSRP on its launch date.

For each GPU, we fit both curves to the observed data and then blended them
together, weighting each in proportion to how well it matched the historical
prices. This blending -- called an Akaike-weighted ensemble -- is more
robust than picking a single curve, because it hedges against either shape
being wrong. If the exponential fit the data much better, the blend leans
heavily toward it but still carries a small contribution from the power law.

Not every GPU has enough data for an individual fit. We built a three-tier
system to handle varying data richness.

Tier one is for GPUs with eighteen or more months of history starting
near their launch date. These get their own personalized curves fit to their
own price data. Examples include the T4, the RTX 3080, and the Quadro RTX
4000. Their projections carry the highest confidence.

Tier two is for GPUs with some data but not enough for a fully independent
fit. We borrow the general decay rate from the GPU's category peers --
datacenter, workstation, or consumer -- and let the price floor adjust to
match this specific GPU's observed prices. Think of it as knowing how cars
in general depreciate, then adjusting for one specific car's current
condition. Tier two now also uses forward projection from the current market
price -- a conditional forecasting approach that anchors the curve to where
the GPU is actually trading today, rather than relying solely on the
historical trajectory. This matters most for GPUs whose recent prices have
diverged sharply from their earlier trend. For very young GPUs like the
H100, which has not yet shown visible depreciation, we use a statistical
technique called an empirical Bayes prior to prevent the model from naively
assuming the GPU will hold ninety-five percent of its value forever. The
prior is built from how older datacenter GPUs in the same category actually
aged, which pulls the H100's projected floor toward a more realistic
thirty-to-forty percent rather than the uninformative ninety-five percent
the raw data would suggest.

Tier three is for GPUs with almost no data -- fewer than four months of
observations, or where all fitting attempts produced implausible results.
These simply receive the average depreciation curve for their category,
scaled to their current observed price level.

Every GPU also gets an uncertainty estimate. We ran ten thousand slightly
varied versions of each curve, sampling from the statistical uncertainty in
the fitted parameters. The final projection is the median of those ten
thousand scenarios. The confidence interval spans the middle ninety-five
percent of outcomes (using the Highest Posterior Density interval). Wide
intervals mean the model genuinely does not know; narrow intervals mean it
is fairly confident.

One additional refinement: when NVIDIA announces a successor GPU generation,
prices for the current generation typically drop twenty to forty percent
within weeks. This is a discrete market event, not smooth depreciation, and
including post-announcement data in the curve fit would inflate the estimated
decay rate. We built a database of fifty successor announcement dates
and, where sufficient pre-announcement data exists, fit the depreciation
curve using only the pre-announcement window. This isolates the structural
depreciation rate from the announcement step-change.


THE DEPRECIATION RANKING

We ranked every GPU on three metrics and combined them into a composite
score. Demand-shocked GPUs are included in the ranking but also receive
separate scenario analysis. The GPU ranked number one is the worst
depreciator.

The first metric is the terminal value ratio -- the projected price at year
five divided by the original MSRP. Lower means worse. The second is dollar
loss -- the actual cash value lost over five years. A nine-thousand-dollar
GPU losing eighty-five percent of its value loses far more in absolute terms
than a three-hundred-dollar card losing the same percentage. The third is the
area under the depreciation curve (AUDC), capped at one point zero, which
captures the total value experienced across all five years rather than just
the endpoint. A GPU that holds its value for three years then crashes scores
differently than one that crashes immediately, even if both end up at the
same five-year price.

Half-life is still reported as a display column but is no longer part of the
composite score. It was removed because GPUs with NaN half-life (observed
method or slow depreciators where floor exceeds fifty percent) would get a
composite mean across three ranks while others get a mean across four, making
scores non-comparable.

Each GPU also receives a reliability rating (HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW) based on
both the fit tier and the confidence flag. HIGH requires Tier 1 data and a
tight confidence interval. A confidence weight is also provided (Tier 1 =
one point zero, Tier 2 = zero point seven, Tier 3 = zero point four) so
downstream consumers can filter or weight by data quality. The MI210 has a
special confidence guard: if it has fewer than twelve months of data or an
age span below eighteen months, its reliability is capped at MEDIUM.

The worst depreciators are uniformly old datacenter and workstation GPUs.
The Tesla V100 family and other previous-generation datacenter accelerators
consistently show the steepest depreciation, retaining only a small fraction
of their original value at the five-year mark. These are GPUs that have been
made obsolete by multiple successor generations and lack the hobbyist or
enthusiast secondary market that cushions consumer cards. A gamer might buy
a five-year-old RTX 3080 for nostalgia or budget reasons; almost nobody
buys a five-year-old V100 unless they have legacy software that
specifically requires it.

At the other end, current-generation professional and enterprise GPUs with
active deployment bases tend to retain the most value, benefiting from
sustained secondary-market demand driven by professional user bases and
enterprise deployment pipelines.

Many consumer GPUs -- particularly the RTX 30-series and several RTX
40-series models -- are currently trading at or above their original MSRP.
This reflects AI-driven demand bleeding into the consumer market in 2025 and
2026. Their long-run depreciation will likely be steeper once that demand
normalizes. Their five-year projections carry low confidence for this reason.


THE REFURBISHMENT PREMIUM

We also investigated whether refurbished GPUs command a price premium over
equivalent used units. The answer is yes, and it is statistically robust.

The primary test was an ordinary least squares regression on the logarithm
of price, controlling for GPU model, time period, and country. The refurbished
coefficient translates to a premium of approximately nineteen percent over
used -- meaning a GPU that sells for one thousand dollars in used condition
would sell for roughly eleven hundred ninety dollars in refurbished condition.
The statistical significance is very high, with a p-value below one in ten
thousand.

As a robustness check, we ran a cluster bootstrap that resamples entire GPU
models rather than individual listings, preserving the temporal correlation
within each GPU's price history. As a further decomposition, we ran
individual t-tests for each GPU with enough data, applying a
Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction to account for multiple
comparisons. Thirty-nine out of forty-five tested GPUs individually showed a
statistically significant positive premium after correction.

The refurbishment premium is analogous to the certified pre-owned premium in
the automotive market. Buyers are paying for inspection, testing, and
warranty coverage -- reduced risk, not improved hardware. For businesses
deploying GPUs in production where downtime from a defective card is costly,
the nineteen percent premium is easily justified. For hobbyists comfortable
testing hardware themselves, buying used and saving roughly one-fifth of the
price is a reasonable trade.


DEMAND SHOCK GPUs

The H100 and H200 families are included in the main depreciation ranking
but flagged as demand-shocked GPUs with separate scenario analysis. The
H100 SXM5 variants are trading at several times their original MSRP on the
secondary market.

This is driven by the convergence of explosive demand for AI training
compute, a manufacturing bottleneck that cannot be resolved quickly, and
enterprise buyers willing to pay almost any price to secure capacity ahead of
competitors. Their current pricing behaviour is fundamentally different from normal
depreciation, driven by AI compute scarcity.

We modeled two scenarios for these GPUs. In the first, the demand premium persists
and the GPU decays gradually from its current elevated price toward the
five-year mark. In the second, the price normalizes to MSRP as supply
catches up with demand, then decays normally from there. The true outcome
will land somewhere between. For conservative financial planning, the
normalization scenario is the appropriate assumption.


EXTENDED HORIZONS AND SUPPLEMENTARY ANALYSIS

Projections now extend to one hundred twenty months (ten years), up from
the original sixty-month window. The output includes projected values at
every twelve-month interval from zero to one hundred twenty months
(proj_t0 through proj_t120). The probability of falling below ten percent
of MSRP is reported at the five-year, eight-year, and ten-year marks.

As a supplementary validation, we implemented a hierarchical Bayesian model
using PyMC. This model places segment-level hyperpriors on the decay
parameters and allows GPU-level partial pooling, so that sparse-data GPUs
borrow strength from their segment peers in a principled way. The
hierarchical Bayes results show a zero point nine four eight correlation
with the main scipy-based pipeline results, confirming the robustness of
the primary methodology. The hierarchical model produces better estimates
for GPUs with very sparse data, where the frequentist approach must fall
back to segment defaults.


POWERPOINT DECK

The pipeline produces a PowerPoint presentation (make_deck.py) containing
one slide per datacenter GPU chart sorted by TVR ascending (worst
depreciators first), followed by family-level charts (Tesla V100, A100,
H100, RTX 30-series, etc.) and segment overview charts (datacenter,
workstation, consumer). Each per-GPU slide shows the GPU name, MSRP,
TVR, five-year residual value in dollars, percentage loss, total data
points, and data sources. The output is outputs/datacenter_gpu_deck.pptx.


MONTE CARLO VALIDATION

Two test scripts validate the Monte Carlo methodology:

mc_convergence_test.py compares two thousand versus ten thousand MC
samples across five representative GPUs (one per fitting pathway: Tier 1
fitted, Tier 1 observed, Tier 2 constrained, Tier 3 default, and
demand-shocked). It measures the coefficient of variation of CI
percentiles across ten runs with different random seeds. The result
confirmed that ten thousand samples produce stable CI estimates.

test_mc_symmetry.py tests whether the MC posterior predictive distribution
is approximately symmetric in log-space, which justifies using a normal
approximation for the uncertainty bands. For each test GPU at each time
point, it compares level-space versus log-space skewness and CI arm
asymmetry.


BACK-TESTING AND VALIDATION

The model has been validated using temporal holdout tests. Four GPUs with
sufficient history were tested by withholding the last six months of data,
fitting on the remainder, and comparing the projected value against the
actual observed price:

    T4:                 projected 0.159, actual 0.153 (+3.9% error)
    V100 PCIe 32 GB:    projected within 95% CI
    A10:                projected within 95% CI
    RTX 3090:           projected 1.13, actual 1.13 (+0.7% error)

All four actual values fell within their respective 95% credible intervals.
The RTX 3090 result is notable: with Keepa data extending the training
window from twenty-two months to the full lifecycle (ages three to sixty
months), the validation error dropped from +748% (retail-only data, no
Keepa) to +0.7%.

As a supplementary validation, a hierarchical Bayesian model implemented in
PyMC produces independent estimates from a fundamentally different
statistical framework. The Pearson correlation between the two models is
0.948 across all GPUs, confirming the robustness of the primary methodology.

A sensitivity analysis (sensitivity_analysis.py) tests stability by varying
four key pipeline thresholds one at a time across their plausible ranges:
TIER1_MIN_MONTHS, OBS_WINDOW, APPRECIATING_THRESHOLD, and
TIER1_MAX_MIN_AGE. The depreciation ranking is robust across all tested
threshold variations, with Spearman rank correlation exceeding 0.965 for
every scenario.


WHAT WE KNOW WE DON'T KNOW

The analysis has clear limitations and we believe it is important to state
them directly.

Newer GPUs have less data. An H200 with six months of history gets a
category-average projection, not an individualized one. Always check the
confidence flag before acting on a number.

Datacenter GPUs are our weakest area. Without Amazon historical pricing, they
rely on only twenty-two months of retail data. Their projections lean on
category averages and should be treated as rough guides.

The model assumes smooth, continuous price decline. In reality, GPU prices
drop suddenly when a successor is announced, when tariffs change, or when a
mining cycle ends. We account for successor announcements explicitly, but
other shocks are not modeled. The curves represent the average expected path;
real prices will occasionally jump well above or below them.

Once trading volume dries up, we lose visibility. The price floor we estimate
reflects GPUs that are still actively changing hands. Units that have been
scrapped or retired may be worth less than our floor suggests.

We measure asset value depreciation only, not total cost of ownership. A GPU
that holds its value well but draws four hundred watts may cost more to
operate over five years than one that depreciates faster but sips one hundred
fifty watts. Electricity and cooling costs are not included in this analysis.

The pipeline underwent iterative refinement. Thresholds for data sufficiency,
model quality gates, and parameter bounds were adjusted during development
based on the data. While each adjustment was individually well-motivated, the
cumulative effect is that results should be interpreted with awareness that
the methodology was not pre-registered. Sensitivity analysis across
threshold values has been performed and shows the ranking is robust
(Spearman rho greater than 0.965 across all tested threshold variations).


HOW MUCH DOES ADDITIONAL DATA HELP?

We ran the pipeline twice -- once with only the twenty-two-month retail
window, and once with the full dataset including Amazon historical prices and
secondary market data from enterprise resellers and eBay. The difference was
dramatic for expensive datacenter GPUs and negligible for consumer cards.

With retail data alone, the H200 NVL was projected to retain ninety-seven
percent of its value -- essentially the model admitting it had no information.
With the full dataset, the projection dropped to thirty-five percent, a
sixty-four percent downward revision. The H100 SXM5 64 GB went from
fifty-nine percent to eighteen percent. All ten of the largest changes were
downward, meaning the retail-only data systematically overstates how well
datacenter GPUs hold their value.

Consumer GPUs barely changed. The RTX 3080, RTX 3090, and T4 moved by less
than two percent because they already had extensive price histories from
Amazon tracking. The external data matters precisely where the pipeline was
weakest -- the expensive datacenter hardware that lacks Amazon price history.

Fifteen of fifty-six GPUs saw their projections shift by more than ten
percentage points. Seven GPUs changed confidence rating. The mean revision
across all GPUs was about seven and a half percentage points, but this
average masks the concentration: datacenter GPUs moved by an average of nine
percentage points while consumer GPUs moved by one.


SUMMARY

Most GPUs lose fifty to eighty-five percent of their value within five years.
The decline follows a predictable pattern: steep in the first two years, then
leveling off at a floor where residual demand sustains a minimum price.

Datacenter GPUs depreciate the hardest. Previous-generation datacenter
accelerators like the V100 family retain only a small fraction of their
original value at the five-year mark, driven by rapid generational
obsolescence and the absence of an enthusiast secondary market.

Current-generation professional GPUs with active enterprise demand tend to
hold value best, sustained by loyal professional user bases and ongoing
deployment pipelines.

The H100 and H200 are not normal depreciation stories right now. They are
trading at multiples of MSRP due to AI compute scarcity. This will not last
indefinitely.

Refurbished GPUs cost about nineteen percent more than equivalent used units.
The premium buys inspection, warranty, and reduced risk. Whether it is worth
paying depends on the buyer's tolerance for hardware failure.

Product cycle timing matters as much as the depreciation curve. When a
successor generation is announced, prices for the previous generation drop
twenty to forty percent within weeks. Anyone planning a significant purchase
or sale should track the product roadmap calendar alongside any
depreciation-based forecast.
